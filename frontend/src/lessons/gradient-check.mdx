## Silent bugs

You now have two ways to get a gradient:

- **Analytic**: derive a formula with the derivative rules and the chain rule. Fast and exact, *if* you got it right.
- **Numerical**: finite differences. Slow, slightly approximate, but almost impossible to get wrong.

Hand-derived gradients are notoriously easy to get wrong: a dropped factor of 2, a flipped sign, a forgotten chain-rule link. Worse, a network with a buggy gradient usually doesn't crash. It just trains badly, or slowly, and you have no idea why.

The fix is the <Term t="gradient check">gradient check</Term>: compute both versions and compare them. If they agree, your analytic gradient is almost certainly correct. You'll gradient-check every layer you write in Modules 5 and 6.

## How to compare two gradients

Comparing gradients with a plain difference is misleading. A difference of $0.01$ is a disaster if the gradient is about $0.02$, and nothing if it's about $1000$. Instead, use the **relative error**, which divides by the sizes of the vectors:

$$
\text{relative error} = \frac{\|\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{n}\|}{\|\mathbf{a}\| + \|\mathbf{n}\|}
$$

where $\mathbf{a}$ is the analytic gradient and $\mathbf{n}$ the numerical one. It's 0 when they're identical, and at most 1. (If both are zero vectors, call the error 0.)

As a rule of thumb, with 64-bit floats and $h \approx 10^{-5}$:

| Relative error | Verdict |
| --- | --- |
| below $10^{-7}$ | 🎉 correct |
| $10^{-7}$ to $10^{-4}$ | suspicious: check for kinks (like ReLU at 0) |
| above $10^{-4}$ | almost certainly a bug |

## A worked example

The Rosenbrock function is a classic test for optimizers, with a long curved valley:

$$
f(x, y) = (1 - x)^2 + 100\,(y - x^2)^2
$$

With the chain rule:

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = -2(1 - x) - 400\,x\,(y - x^2), \qquad
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 200\,(y - x^2)
$$

At $(-1, 2)$ that gives $\nabla f = (396, 200)$. A numerical gradient with $h = 10^{-5}$ gives the same thing to about 9 digits, for a relative error around $10^{-11}$. Correct.

Now suppose you'd forgotten the factor of 2 in the second term and written $-200\,x\,(y - x^2)$. You'd get $\nabla f = (196, 200)$, a relative error of about $0.28$. Caught immediately.

<Callout type="tip" title="Gradient checking in practice">

- Use 64-bit floats (`double` / `f64`). 32-bit floats are too imprecise for finite differences.
- For a big network, don't check every weight (that's $2n$ forward passes). Check a few dozen at random.
- Turn gradient checking off once you're confident: it's a debugging tool, not part of training.

</Callout>

<KeyIdea>

Never trust a hand-derived gradient until it has passed a gradient check: compare it with a numerical gradient using the relative error $\|\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{n}\| / (\|\mathbf{a}\| + \|\mathbf{n}\|)$.

</KeyIdea>
