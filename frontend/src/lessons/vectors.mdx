## A list of numbers…

A <Term>vector</Term> is an ordered list of numbers. That's the whole definition:

$$
\mathbf{v} = (3,\ 1) \qquad \mathbf{w} = (0.2,\ -1.5,\ 4,\ 7)
$$

The numbers are its **components**, written $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$, and how many there are is its **dimension**. $\mathbf{v}$ above is 2-dimensional; $\mathbf{w}$ is 4-dimensional. A single number on its own is called a <Term>scalar</Term>.

In machine learning, almost everything is a vector:

- A house might be `(square metres, bedrooms, age)`: a 3-dimensional vector.
- An MNIST image is a vector of **784** pixel brightnesses.
- A neuron's <Term t="weight">weights</Term> are a vector, one number per input.
- The network's output is a vector of 10 probabilities, one per digit.

## …or an arrow

In two or three dimensions you can also *draw* a vector: an arrow from the origin to the point $(v_1, v_2)$. The arrow picture gives you intuition that carries over to 784 dimensions, even though nobody can picture those.

## Adding vectors

Vectors of the same dimension add **component by component**:

$$
\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} = (a_1 + b_1,\ a_2 + b_2,\ \dots,\ a_n + b_n)
$$

$$
(3,\ 1) + (1,\ 2) = (4,\ 3)
$$

Geometrically, that's **tip to tail**: walk along $\mathbf{a}$, then from where you end up, walk along $\mathbf{b}$. Drag the arrows above and watch the dashed parallelogram: you reach the same corner whichever order you walk, which is why $\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{a}$.

What's $(3, 1) + (1, 2, 5)$? It isn't anything: the operation only makes sense when the dimensions match. Your code will have to **reject** mismatched lengths instead of quietly returning something wrong. Shape errors like this are the most common bug in neural-network code, and catching them early saves hours.

## Scaling a vector

Multiplying a vector by a scalar $s$ multiplies every component:

$$
s\,\mathbf{v} = (s v_1,\ s v_2,\ \dots,\ s v_n) \qquad 2\,(3,\ 1) = (6,\ 2)
$$

The arrow keeps its direction but gets $s$ times longer. A scalar between 0 and 1 shrinks it, and a **negative** scalar flips it to point the opposite way.

## Why these two matter

Adding and scaling look almost too simple, but together they're how neural networks **learn**. Training repeatedly updates a weight vector $\mathbf{w}$ with a rule that looks like this:

$$
\mathbf{w}_{\text{new}} = \mathbf{w} + (-\eta)\,\mathbf{g}
$$

Scale the gradient $\mathbf{g}$ by a small negative number, then add it to the weights. You'll meet $\mathbf{g}$ (the gradient) in Module 2 and $\eta$ (the learning rate) in Module 4, but the operations are the two you're about to write.

<KeyIdea>

A vector is a list of numbers, and also an arrow. Vectors of the same length add component by component (tip to tail). Multiplying by a scalar stretches, shrinks or flips the arrow.

</KeyIdea>
