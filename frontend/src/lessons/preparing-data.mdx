## Standardizing the inputs

Your pixels are now numbers in $[0, 1]$, but they aren't spread evenly: most of every image is blank background. Over the whole training set, the mean pixel value is $0.1307$ and the standard deviation is $0.3081$.

Before training, every pixel is <Term t="standardization">standardized</Term>:

$$
x' = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} = \frac{x - 0.1307}{0.3081}
$$

so the inputs have mean 0 and standard deviation 1. There are two reasons, both of which you've met before:

- **Initialization assumes it.** He initialization (Module 3) picks the weights' spread so that signals keep roughly the same size from layer to layer, and that calculation assumes inputs with variance about 1.
- **Gradient descent likes round valleys.** If every input is positive, the gradients for all of a neuron's weights are pushed the same way at once, and the loss surface becomes a long, narrow valley like the one in the momentum lesson. Centring the inputs makes the valley rounder, so plain gradient descent goes more directly downhill.

  id="prep-ink"
  question="What does a pixel of full ink (1.0) become after standardizing with μ = 0.1307 and σ = 0.3081? (Two decimal places is enough.)"
  answer={2.8215}

/>

<Callout type="warning" title="Use the training set's numbers everywhere">

The mean and standard deviation come from the **training** images, and the same two numbers are then used for the test images and for anything you draw yourself. Computing them separately on the test set would quietly use information the model isn't supposed to have. And if the inputs are prepared differently from what the network was trained on, its predictions are meaningless.

</Callout>

## Mini-batches by index

Training uses mini-batch stochastic gradient descent from Module 4. Each <Term>epoch</Term>:

1. Shuffle the numbers $0, 1, \dots, n-1$ with your `shuffled_indices` (Module 3).
2. Walk through them in chunks of the batch size. Each chunk is one <Term>mini-batch</Term>.
3. For each chunk, pull out those images and labels, compute the average gradient over them, and take one step.

```text
order = [41203, 7, 5590, 18, 33017, ...]        shuffled 0 .. 59,999
batch 0 = rows order[0 .. 31],   batch 1 = rows order[32 .. 63],   ...
```

Shuffling indices instead of the data is much cheaper: 60,000 integers instead of 47 million pixels. It also keeps every label with its image automatically, because images and labels are gathered with the **same** indices.
