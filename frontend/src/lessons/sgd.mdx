## Full-batch is expensive

To take one step of gradient descent you need the gradient of the loss, which is an average over **every** training example. For MNIST that means running all 60,000 images through the network, and back, just to move the weights once. Training needs thousands of steps.

## Estimate the gradient instead

The loss is an average, so its gradient is an average too:

$$
\nabla L = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \nabla \ell_i
$$

The average over a small random sample, a <Term>mini-batch</Term> of say 64 examples, is a decent **estimate** of the average over all of them, like the sample mean from Module 3. It's noisy, but on average it points the right way, and it costs 1,000 times less to compute. That's <Term t="stochastic gradient descent">stochastic gradient descent</Term> (SGD): take many cheap, noisy steps instead of a few expensive, exact ones.

<LineFitViz mode="sgd" />

Smaller batches mean noisier steps but more of them per epoch. In practice, batches of 32 to 256 are typical. A bit of noise is even helpful: it can bounce the weights out of poor flat spots that full-batch gradient descent would settle into.

## Shuffling

If the batches always contained the same examples in the same order, the noise wouldn't be random anymore. Worse, if the data were sorted (all the 0s first, then the 1s…), each batch would see only one digit and the network would lurch from one class to the next. So training **shuffles** the data at the start of every epoch.

The right way to shuffle is the <Term t="fisher-yates shuffle">Fisher–Yates shuffle</Term>:

```text
for i from n−1 down to 1:
    j = a random integer between 0 and i (inclusive)
    swap items i and j
```

Every one of the $n!$ possible orders is equally likely. A tempting shortcut, swapping each item with a random position anywhere in the list, looks similar but is **biased**: some orders come up noticeably more often than others.

With your own `xorshift32` generating the random $j$, every language produces exactly the same shuffle for the same seed.

<KeyIdea>

SGD estimates the gradient from a small random mini-batch: noisy but cheap, so you can take many more steps. Each epoch, shuffle the data (Fisher–Yates) and walk through it one batch at a time.
