## Many inputs, one output

A network's loss doesn't depend on one weight. It depends on all of them at once, thousands or millions of numbers. So you need derivatives of functions with **many inputs**, like this one:

$$
f(x, y) = x^2 y + 3y
$$

## Partial derivatives

The idea: change one input at a time. The <Term t="partial derivative">partial derivative</Term> $\partial f / \partial x$ is the ordinary derivative with respect to $x$ while you treat every other input **as a constant**:

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2xy \qquad \text{(treat } y \text{ as a constant)}
$$

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x^2 + 3 \qquad \text{(treat } x \text{ as a constant)}
$$

Geometrically, $\partial f / \partial x$ is the slope of the surface if you walk in the $x$ direction only, and $\partial f / \partial y$ is the slope walking in the $y$ direction only.

## The gradient

Collect all the partial derivatives into a vector and you get the <Term>gradient</Term>, written $\nabla f$ ("nabla f" or "del f"):

$$
\nabla f(x, y) = \begin{bmatrix} \partial f / \partial x \\ \partial f / \partial y \end{bmatrix}
$$

The gradient has a beautiful geometric meaning: **it points in the direction of steepest ascent**, and its length says how steep that is. It's always perpendicular to the contour lines, the lines of equal height.

<GradientField />

Drag the point around each surface. The blue and purple arrows are the two partial derivatives, and the orange arrow is their sum, the gradient. Then try **Step downhill** a few times.

That green dashed arrow, pointing **opposite** the gradient, is the direction of steepest *descent*. Repeatedly stepping that way is <Term>gradient descent</Term>, the algorithm that trains essentially every neural network:

$$
\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta\,\nabla L(\mathbf{w})
$$

You'll build it properly in Module 4. While you're here, try the narrow valley with a big step size, and watch gradient descent zig-zag or even fly off. Choosing the step size well matters a lot.

## Computing gradients numerically

Every partial derivative is an ordinary derivative along one axis, so a finite difference works for each one. To nudge only coordinate $i$, add $h$ times the **unit vector** $\mathbf{e}_i$ (all zeros except a 1 in position $i$):

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} \approx \frac{f(\mathbf{x} + h\,\mathbf{e}_i) - f(\mathbf{x} - h\,\mathbf{e}_i)}{2h}
$$

For $n$ inputs that's $2n$ evaluations of $f$: fine for checking a few weights, hopeless for training millions. Backpropagation gets the whole gradient for roughly the cost of *one* evaluation.

<Callout type="note" title="Vector-valued functions">

If a function has many inputs *and* many outputs, like a layer turning a vector into a vector, every output has its own gradient. Stacked as rows, they form a matrix called the **Jacobian**. For a linear layer $\mathbf{z} = W\mathbf{x}$, the Jacobian with respect to $\mathbf{x}$ is just $W$, which is why gradients flowing backwards through it get multiplied by $W^\mathsf{T}$. More on that in Module 5.

</Callout>

<KeyIdea>

A partial derivative varies one input and holds the rest fixed. The gradient $\nabla f$ collects them into a vector that points straight uphill. Gradient descent steps the opposite way.

</KeyIdea>
