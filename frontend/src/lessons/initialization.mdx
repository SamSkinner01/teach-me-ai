## The bell curve

Uniform numbers spread evenly between 0 and 1. For initializing weights you'll want a different shape: the <Term t="normal distribution">normal distribution</Term>, the famous bell curve. Values cluster around a mean $\mu$, and become rarer the further out you go:

$$
p(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}
$$

It's written $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$. About 68% of samples land within one standard deviation $\sigma$ of the mean, 95% within two, and 99.7% within three. The **standard** normal $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ has mean 0 and standard deviation 1, and any other normal is just a stretched and shifted copy: if $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, then $\mu + \sigma z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$.

Normals show up everywhere because adding up many small independent random effects tends to produce one (the *central limit theorem*). For us, what matters is that it's the standard way to draw random starting weights.

## From uniform to normal: Box–Muller

Your generator makes uniform numbers. The <Term t="box-muller transform">Box–Muller transform</Term> (1958) turns two independent uniforms $u_1, u_2$ into two independent standard normals:

$$
r = \sqrt{-2\ln u_1}, \qquad \theta = 2\pi u_2, \qquad z_1 = r\cos\theta, \qquad z_2 = r\sin\theta
$$

Here's the intuition. A pair of independent standard normals, plotted as a point in the plane, is a round cloud: the direction is equally likely to be anything, so $\theta$ is just a uniform angle. The distance from the origin has its own distribution, and $\sqrt{-2\ln u_1}$ is exactly the formula that turns a uniform number into a distance with that distribution. Since $u_1$ is never exactly 0, the logarithm is always finite.

## Why not start every weight at zero?

It seems natural to start with all weights at zero, or all equal. It doesn't work. If every neuron in a layer starts with the same weights, they all compute the same output and receive the same gradient, so they all update identically, and stay identical forever. A layer of 100 neurons would act like a single neuron. Random initialization **breaks the symmetry**, so different neurons can learn different features.

## How big should the random weights be?

Random, yes, but drawn with what standard deviation? Think about one neuron with $n$ inputs:

$$
z = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \dots + w_n x_n
$$

If the weights and inputs are independent with mean 0, the variances of the terms add up:

$$
\mathrm{Var}[z] = n \cdot \mathrm{Var}[w] \cdot \mathrm{Var}[x]
$$

So each layer multiplies the variance of the signal by $n \cdot \mathrm{Var}[w]$. In a deep network that factor gets applied over and over:

- If $n\,\mathrm{Var}[w] < 1$, the signal shrinks geometrically and **vanishes**. Gradients flowing backwards shrink the same way (the <Term t="vanishing gradient">vanishing gradient</Term> problem), so the early layers barely learn.
- If $n\,\mathrm{Var}[w] > 1$, the signal grows geometrically and **explodes**, or with tanh, everything saturates at $\pm 1$, where the gradient is zero.
- If $n\,\mathrm{Var}[w] = 1$, the signal keeps its size all the way through.

That gives **Xavier** (or LeCun) initialization: $\mathrm{Var}[w] = 1/n$, i.e. $\sigma = \sqrt{1/n}$. Here $n$ is the layer's <Term t="fan-in">fan-in</Term>, its number of inputs.

ReLU needs one more correction: it zeroes every negative input, roughly half of them, which halves the variance at every layer. To compensate, double the weight variance. That's <Term t="he initialization">He initialization</Term> (Kaiming He et al., 2015), the standard choice for ReLU networks like your CNN:

$$
w \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\ \frac{2}{n}\right) \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{\frac{2}{n}}
$$

<KeyIdea>

Box–Muller turns pairs of uniforms into standard normals. Weights start random to break symmetry, with variance chosen so that $n \cdot \mathrm{Var}[w]$ keeps the signal's size steady through the layers: $1/n$ (Xavier) for tanh, $2/n$ (He) for ReLU.

</KeyIdea>
