## More than two dimensions

Vectors are 1-dimensional lists and matrices are 2-dimensional grids. Keep going and you get **<Term t="tensor">tensors</Term>**: arrays with any number of axes.

| Tensor | Axes | Example | Shape |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| scalar | 0 | a loss value | `[]` |
| vector | 1 | a neuron's weights | `[784]` |
| matrix | 2 | one grayscale image | `[28, 28]` |
| 3-D tensor | 3 | one colour image (red, green, blue) | `[3, 32, 32]` |
| 4-D tensor | 4 | a batch of 64 MNIST images | `[64, 1, 28, 28]` |

A tensor's <Term>shape</Term> lists its size along each axis. The total number of elements is the product of the shape: a `[2, 3, 4]` tensor has $2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 = 24$ numbers.

Your CNN will be full of 4-D tensors. Its convolution filters alone have shape `[out channels, in channels, height, width]`.

## Memory is flat

Here's the catch: computer memory isn't a grid. It's one long line of numbered slots. Every tensor, however many axes it has, is stored as **one flat block of numbers**, and its shape is just a rule for finding things in that block.

The standard rule is <Term>row-major</Term> order: lay out the last axis first. For a matrix, that means row 0 left to right, then row 1, and so on. For a `[2, 3, 4]` tensor, all of `[0, 0, :]`, then `[0, 1, :]`, … then `[1, 2, :]`. Equivalently, **the last index changes fastest**, like the digits of an odometer.

## Strides

How do you find element `[i, j, k]` in the flat block? Ask how far you jump in memory when each index goes up by one. That jump is the axis's <Term>stride</Term>.

- Moving one step along the **last** axis moves to the next slot: stride 1.
- Moving one step along the **second-to-last** axis skips a whole row of the last axis: stride `shape[-1]`.
- In general, each stride is the next one multiplied by the next axis's size:

$$
\text{stride}_{\text{last}} = 1, \qquad \text{stride}_i = \text{stride}_{i+1} \times \text{shape}_{i+1}
$$

A `[2, 3, 4]` tensor has strides `[12, 4, 1]`. The flat position of any element is then a dot product of its index with the strides:

$$
\text{flat}(i, j, k) = 12i + 4j + 1k
$$

## Reshaping is free

Since the shape is only a way of *reading* the flat block, you can change it without moving any data, as long as the total size stays the same. A `[2, 3, 4]` tensor can be read as `[6, 4]`, `[4, 6]`, `[24]` or `[2, 12]`, and the 24 numbers stay exactly where they are.

**Flattening** is the special case of reshaping to one long axis. Your CNN does exactly this: its last convolution produces a `[16, 5, 5]` tensor, which is flattened to 400 numbers before the final layer computes its dot products.

</Callout>

<KeyIdea>

A tensor is a flat block of numbers plus a shape. Row-major strides say how far to jump per axis: the last stride is 1, and each earlier stride is the next one times the next size. Flat position = index · strides. Reshaping changes the shape, not the data.

</KeyIdea>
