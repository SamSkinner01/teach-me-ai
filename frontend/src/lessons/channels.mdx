## Many filters, many channels

One filter detects one pattern. A real convolution layer has many filters, say 8 or 64, and each produces its own feature map. Stack those maps and the layer's output is a 3-D tensor of shape $[C_{\text{out}}, H, W]$: one **<Term t="channel">channel</Term>** per filter.

The next layer's input therefore has several channels too, and so does a colour photo (red, green and blue). So a filter in a real layer isn't a flat $k \times k$ grid. It has one $k \times k$ slice **per input channel**, and its output adds up the convolutions of every input channel with its own slice:

$$
\text{out}[o, i, j] = b_o + \sum_{c} \sum_{u,v} W[o, c, u, v]\; x[c,\ i+u,\ j+v]
$$

So a layer's weights form a 4-D tensor of shape $[C_{\text{out}}, C_{\text{in}}, k, k]$, plus one bias per output channel.

## Storing tensors flat

How do you store a $[C, H, W]$ tensor? Exactly as Module 1 taught: one flat array in row-major order, with strides $[HW,\ W,\ 1]$. Element $(c, i, j)$ lives at index

$$
(c \cdot H + i)\cdot W + j
$$

and a weight $(o, c, u, v)$ in a $[O, C, K, K]$ tensor at $((o \cdot C + c)\cdot K + u)\cdot K + v$. Real frameworks store tensors exactly like this. In the next exercise, tensors come in as a flat vector plus a shape, just as they will in your capstone CNN.

<KeyIdea>

A convolution layer has $C_{\text{out}}$ filters, each with one $k\times k$ slice per input channel; output channel $o$ is its bias plus the sum, over input channels, of each channel convolved with its slice. Weights form a $[C_{\text{out}}, C_{\text{in}}, k, k]$ tensor, and everything is stored flat and row-major.

</KeyIdea>
