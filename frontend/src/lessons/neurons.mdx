## A neuron, in math

Artificial neurons were loosely inspired by brain cells, but the math is simple. A <Term>neuron</Term> takes a vector of inputs $\mathbf{x}$, computes a weighted sum plus a bias, and passes the result through an <Term t="activation function">activation function</Term> $\varphi$:

$$
a = \varphi(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b)
$$

You've met every piece: the dot product (Module 1), the sigmoid (Module 2), and how to train weights (Module 4). With $\varphi = \sigma$, a single neuron is a complete classifier called **logistic regression**: it outputs the probability that $\mathbf{x}$ belongs to class 1.

## One neuron draws one line

Where does a neuron switch from "class 0" to "class 1"? Where $\sigma(z) = \tfrac12$, which happens exactly when $z = \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b = 0$. For two inputs that's the equation of a **straight line**; in more dimensions, a flat plane. So whatever its weights, one neuron can only ever separate classes with a straight boundary.

Some problems simply aren't separable by a line. The classic example is **XOR**: output 1 when exactly one of two inputs is on. Try it above. You can't get more than 6 of the 8 points right, and the 1969 discovery of this limitation famously stalled neural-network research for years. The fix is to use more than one layer of neurons.

## Why the activation function matters

Here's a surprise. Stack two layers **without** an activation function and you gain nothing:

$$
W_2(W_1\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_1) + \mathbf{b}_2 = (W_2W_1)\,\mathbf{x} + (W_2\mathbf{b}_1 + \mathbf{b}_2)
$$

That's just another single linear layer, with weight matrix $W_2W_1$. A product of matrices is still one matrix, so a hundred linear layers are no more powerful than one. The non-linear $\varphi$ between layers is what lets deep networks bend their decision boundaries into any shape.

## Choosing an activation

<ActivationViz />

For hidden layers, the modern default is the <Term>relu</Term> (rectified linear unit):

$$
\mathrm{relu}(z) = \max(0, z), \qquad \mathrm{relu}'(z) = \begin{cases} 1 & z > 0 \\ 0 & z \le 0 \end{cases}
$$

It's almost embarrassingly simple, but it works well. It's cheap to compute, and its slope is exactly 1 for positive inputs, so gradients flow back through many layers without shrinking, unlike the sigmoid, whose slope is at most $\tfrac14$. (At exactly $z = 0$ the slope isn't defined; by convention we use 0.)

## ReLU in the backward pass

During backpropagation, a gradient $\partial L/\partial a$ arrives at the neuron's output, and the chain rule multiplies it by the activation's slope:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial a}\cdot \mathrm{relu}'(z)
$$

For ReLU that's a gate: the gradient passes through unchanged where $z$ was positive, and is blocked (set to 0) where it wasn't. Note that the backward pass needs $z$ from the forward pass, so the network has to remember it.

<KeyIdea>

A neuron computes $\varphi(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b)$, and on its own it can only draw a straight decision boundary. Non-linear activations between layers are what make depth useful. ReLU, $\max(0, z)$, passes gradients through where its input was positive and blocks them elsewhere.

</KeyIdea>
