## The question a network keeps asking

Training a neural network comes down to one question, asked millions of times:

> If I nudge this weight up a tiny bit, does the network's error go up or down, and by how much?

The answer is a **<Term>derivative</Term>**. This module is about computing them: first numerically (this lesson), then exactly with a few rules, and finally for functions built out of other functions, which is what networks are.

## Slopes

For a straight line, the slope is rise over run: how much $y$ changes per unit change in $x$. A slope of 3 means "move right by 1, go up by 3". A curve doesn't have a single slope, but here's the key insight of calculus: **zoom in far enough on any smooth curve and it looks like a straight line**. The slope of that line is the derivative at that point.

## From secant to tangent

To estimate the slope at $x$, take a second point a small step $h$ to the right and compute rise over run between them. The line through two points on a curve is a **secant**:

$$
\text{slope} \approx \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}
$$

As $h$ shrinks, the secant swings round into the **tangent** line, and its slope becomes the derivative:

$$
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}
$$

Drag the $h$ slider in the figure towards zero and watch the orange secant land on the green dashed tangent.

For $f(x) = x^2$ you can take the limit by hand:

$$
\frac{(x+h)^2 - x^2}{h} = \frac{x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - x^2}{h} = 2x + h \;\xrightarrow{\;h \to 0\;}\; 2x
$$

So the derivative of $x^2$ is $2x$: at $x = 3$ the curve climbs with slope 6, and at $x = -2$ it falls with slope $-4$.

## Doing it numerically

The difference quotient with a small, fixed $h$ is a **finite difference**, and it works for *any* function you can evaluate. You don't need a formula. There are two common versions:

$$
\underbrace{\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}}_{\text{forward difference}}
\qquad\qquad
\underbrace{\frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h)}{2h}}_{\text{central difference}}
$$

The central difference looks symmetrically on both sides of $x$, and it's far more accurate: its error shrinks like $h^2$ instead of $h$. Switch the figure to "central" and compare the error readouts. For $x^2$ it's exact for *every* $h$!

<Callout type="warning" title="Smaller isn't always better">

Floating-point numbers have about 16 significant digits. With a tiny $h$ like $10^{-12}$, $f(x+h)$ and $f(x)$ agree in almost every digit, and subtracting them throws most of those digits away. Rounding error swamps the result. In practice $h \approx 10^{-5}$ is a good choice for central differences.

</Callout>

So why not compute every derivative this way? Because it's **slow** for networks. Each finite difference needs extra evaluations of the function, and you'd need one per weight. A network with a million weights would need a million forward passes for a single training step. Instead, networks use exact derivative rules (next lesson) chained together cleverly (the lesson after). Finite differences are still invaluable as a **check** that those exact derivatives are right.

<KeyIdea>

The derivative $f'(x)$ is the slope of the tangent line: the limit of rise over run as the run shrinks to zero. It says how fast $f$ changes when you nudge $x$. Finite differences estimate it numerically; the central version is much more accurate.

</KeyIdea>
