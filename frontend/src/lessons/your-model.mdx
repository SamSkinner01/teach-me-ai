## When it gets your handwriting wrong

It probably will sometimes, even though it scored well on the test set. Your drawings aren't MNIST digits: you draw with a mouse or a finger instead of a pen, your strokes are a different thickness, and your 7s and 1s may follow different conventions from those of 1990s American writers.

This is called <Term t="distribution shift">distribution shift</Term>, and it's the most common reason a model that tests well disappoints in the real world. The test set only predicts performance on data **like the training data**. Experiments to try:

- Draw a digit very small, off-centre or rotated. The preprocessing undoes the size and position, but nothing undoes the rotation.
- Draw a 7 with a cross-bar, or a 1 with a base. Both are common outside the US.
- Switch to the demo model and draw the same thing. It has a second convolution layer and was trained for longer. Is it more reliable on your handwriting, or does it fail on the same drawings?

## What you built

Every number in that network was learned with code you wrote, and every function it calls is one you wrote and tested:

- **Linear algebra**: vectors, dot products, matrix–vector and matrix–matrix products, transposes, reshaping and strides.
- **Calculus**: derivatives, the chain rule, gradients, and numerical gradient checking to make sure they're right.
- **Probability**: a random number generator from scratch, normal samples, softmax and cross-entropy, He initialization.
- **Optimization**: gradient descent, mini-batches and shuffling, momentum.
- **Neural networks**: dense layers, ReLU, and backpropagation through all of it.
- **Convolutional networks**: convolution, pooling and their gradients.
- **The capstone**: a file parser, data preparation, a CNN, a training loop that reads 60,000 real images, and an evaluation.

That's 78 library functions, and together they're a small deep-learning framework.

## Where to go next

What separates this network from the state of the art is mostly scale and a handful of ideas, each a small step from what you know:

- **Deeper networks.** Stack two or more convolution layers, like the demo model (98.4% on the test set), so later layers combine the earlier layers' strokes into shapes.
- **Better optimizers.** Adam, the default in most of deep learning, is momentum plus a separate, automatically adapted step size for each parameter.
- **Regularization.** Data augmentation (training on slightly shifted, rotated and scaled copies of each image) and dropout both fight overfitting.
- **Batch normalization.** It re-standardizes activations inside the network, the way you standardized the inputs.
- **Frameworks and GPUs.** PyTorch and JAX do exactly what your library does, but compute the backward pass for you (automatic differentiation) and run the loops on hardware that does trillions of multiply-adds a second.

When you use one of them, you'll know what each call does inside, because you've written it yourself.
