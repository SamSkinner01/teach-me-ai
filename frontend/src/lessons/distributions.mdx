## Why probability?

So far everything has been exact: a dot product has one right answer. But neural networks live in an uncertain world:

- A classifier doesn't say "that's a 7". It says "I'm 92% sure it's a 7, 5% sure it's a 1, …" Its outputs are **probabilities**.
- Training measures how much probability the network gave to the right answers, and pushes that up.
- Training is full of deliberate randomness: random starting weights, data shuffled into a random order every pass.

This module builds the probability you need for all three, starting with the basics.

## Random variables and distributions

A <Term>random variable</Term> is a number decided by chance, like the result of rolling a die. Its <Term>probability distribution</Term> says which values it can take and how likely each one is:

| value $x$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| probability $p(x)$ | $\tfrac16$ | $\tfrac16$ | $\tfrac16$ | $\tfrac16$ | $\tfrac16$ | $\tfrac16$ |

Two rules every distribution obeys: probabilities are never negative, and they **sum to 1** (something has to happen).

## Expected value

If you rolled that die forever, what would the average roll be? Weight each value by how often it happens:

$$
\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_i x_i\, p_i = 1\cdot\tfrac16 + 2\cdot\tfrac16 + \dots + 6\cdot\tfrac16 = 3.5
$$

That's the <Term t="expected value">expected value</Term> (or mean) of $X$, usually written $\mu$. Notice you can never actually roll a 3.5: the expected value is a long-run average, not a likely outcome.

Look at the formula again: $\sum_i x_i p_i$ is a **dot product** of the values with the probabilities. Your `dot` will do the work.

## Samples settle down

In practice you rarely know the true distribution. You have **samples**: actual rolls, or actual training images. The **sample mean** is just their average, $\bar{x} = \frac1n\sum_i x_i$. The <Term t="law of large numbers">law of large numbers</Term> says that as $n$ grows, the sample mean closes in on the expected value. Try it:

<SamplingViz />

With 10 rolls, the frequencies are all over the place. With 10,000 they hug the true probabilities. The same thing happens when a network measures its error on a large batch of examples: the average over the batch is a good estimate of the average over *all* possible images.

## Variance and standard deviation

Two distributions can have the same mean and look completely different. A die that always lands on 3 or 4 and one that always lands on 1 or 6 both average 3.5. The <Term>variance</Term> measures **spread**: the average squared distance from the mean,

$$
\mathrm{Var}[X] = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mu)^2\big]
$$

For data, the same idea: average the squared deviations from the sample mean. Squaring makes every deviation count positively, and big deviations count a lot. The <Term t="standard deviation">standard deviation</Term> $\sigma = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[X]}$ brings it back to the original units.

<Callout type="note" title="n or n − 1?">

You may have seen variance computed by dividing by $n - 1$ instead of $n$. That version, the *sample* variance with Bessel's correction, is a slightly better estimate of the true variance when $n$ is small. In this course we divide by $n$ (the *population* variance), which is what deep-learning code uses.

</Callout>

## Where this shows up in AI

- **Normalizing inputs.** The demo network in lesson 0 didn't see raw pixel values. It saw $(x - 0.1307)/0.3081$, where $0.1307$ and $0.3081$ are the mean and standard deviation of all MNIST pixels. Centring and scaling inputs like this makes training much easier.
- **Weight initialization.** Starting weights are drawn with a carefully chosen variance. Too big or too small and a deep network can't learn at all. That's the last lesson of this module.
- **Measuring a model.** Accuracy and loss are averages over many examples: sample means.

<KeyIdea>

A distribution lists values and their probabilities (non-negative, summing to 1). The expected value $\sum x_i p_i$ is the long-run average, and sample means converge to it. Variance, the mean squared distance from the mean, measures spread; its square root is the standard deviation.

</KeyIdea>
