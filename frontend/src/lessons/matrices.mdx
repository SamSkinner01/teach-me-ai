## A grid of numbers

A <Term>matrix</Term> is a rectangular grid of numbers. One with $m$ rows and $n$ columns is called an $m \times n$ matrix (rows first, always):

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \qquad (2 \times 3)
$$

The entry in row $i$, column $j$ is written $A_{ij}$, so here $A_{12} = 2$ and $A_{21} = 4$. In code, a matrix is a list of rows, and you get that entry with `A[i][j]`. Code counts from 0, so the maths $A_{12}$ is `A[0][1]`.

## Multiplying a matrix by a vector

You can multiply an $m \times n$ matrix by a vector with $n$ components. The answer has $m$ components, one per row, and each one is a **dot product** of that row with the vector:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} (1, 2)\cdot(5, 6) \\ (3, 4)\cdot(5, 6) \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} 17 \\ 39 \end{bmatrix}
$$

That's the **row view**, and it's how you'll compute it. The number of columns must equal the vector's length, or the dot products don't line up.

## A matrix is a transformation

There's a second, more geometric way to read the same product, the **column view**:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}
= x\begin{bmatrix} a \\ c \end{bmatrix} + y\begin{bmatrix} b \\ d \end{bmatrix}
$$

The output is $x$ copies of the first column plus $y$ copies of the second. Now think about the two <Term t="basis vector">basis vectors</Term> $\hat\imath = (1, 0)$ and $\hat\jmath = (0, 1)$. Multiplying by the matrix sends $\hat\imath$ to the first column and $\hat\jmath$ to the second, and every other point $(x, y) = x\hat\imath + y\hat\jmath$ follows along.

So a matrix is a **function that moves every point in space**, and its columns tell you exactly where the basis vectors land. Those functions are called <Term t="linear transformation">linear transformations</Term>: grid lines stay straight, parallel and evenly spaced, and the origin stays put.

<MatrixTransformer />

Things to try:

- **Rotate 90°**: $\hat\imath$ lands on $(0, 1)$ and $\hat\jmath$ on $(-1, 0)$, so the matrix is $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.
- **Shear**: $\hat\imath$ stays put and $\hat\jmath$ leans over. Squares become parallelograms.
- **Reflect**: the F is mirrored, and the <Term>determinant</Term> goes negative.
- **Squash**: drag $\hat\jmath$ onto the same line as $\hat\imath$. The whole plane collapses onto a line and the determinant becomes 0. Different points now land in the same place, so the transformation can't be undone.

## A layer of neurons is a matrix

One neuron computes a dot product $\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}$. A **layer** of $m$ neurons, all looking at the same input, computes $m$ dot products. Stack the $m$ weight vectors as the rows of a matrix $W$, and the whole layer becomes one matrix-vector product:

$$
\mathbf{z} = W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}
$$

A layer that maps 784 pixels to 64 neurons has a $64 \times 784$ weight matrix: 64 rows (one per neuron) and 784 columns (one per pixel). Each output entry is one neuron's response.

## The transpose

The <Term>transpose</Term> $A^\mathsf{T}$ flips a matrix over its diagonal, so rows become columns:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}^\mathsf{T} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 5 \\ 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix}
\qquad (A^\mathsf{T})_{ij} = A_{ji}
$$

An $m \times n$ matrix becomes $n \times m$. It looks like mere bookkeeping, but it's essential for training: when gradients flow *backwards* through a layer, they're multiplied by $W^\mathsf{T}$ instead of $W$. You'll see why in Module 5.

<KeyIdea>

$A\mathbf{x}$ has one entry per row of $A$: that row dotted with $\mathbf{x}$. Geometrically, a matrix is a transformation whose columns are where the basis vectors land. A neural-network layer is a matrix-vector product.

</KeyIdea>
